Do I Need a Brake Controller?

Five of our six trailers have electric brakes. Here's how to tell what your truck has before you're standing in the driveway with a trailer you can't stop.

HomeBlogDo I Need a Brake Controller?

By Eagle Mountain Trailer Rental LLC · Updated November 2026

This is the question that catches more of my renters off guard than any other, and it almost always comes up at the worst possible moment. Somebody books a trailer, shows up to hook it, plugs in the 7-pin, and finds out their truck has no way to run the trailer's brakes.

So here's the straight answer for every trailer I rent, and how to check your own truck before you're standing in my driveway.

The short version

Five of my six trailers have electric brakes, which means your tow vehicle needs a working brake controller. One doesn't.

The utility trailer is the exception. Single 3,500 lb axle, no electric brakes, and it runs a flat 4-pin plug that doesn't have a brake wire in it at all. If the utility is what you're renting, none of the rest of this applies to you.

What a brake controller actually does

The trailer has its own brakes, on its own axles. The brake controller is the box in your truck that tells those brakes how hard to work when you press the pedal.

Without one, the trailer has brakes it cannot use. You're stopping truck and trailer on the truck's brakes alone. On a loaded dump trailer that's around 14,000 pounds total trying to stop on brakes sized for a fraction of it.

It's also what keeps a trailer from shoving you around on a downhill grade. Anybody who has come down a canyon heavy without trailer brakes doesn't need me to describe the feeling.

How to tell if your truck has one

Three things to look for.

A factory integrated controller. Most 3/4-ton and 1-ton trucks from roughly the last fifteen years have one built in, usually with a gain setting and a manual slide lever somewhere near the dash or center stack. If your truck has a menu item for trailer brake gain, you have one.

An aftermarket box. Normally a small unit mounted under the dash near your left knee, with a dial and a lever. If somebody towed with this truck before you owned it, look down there before you assume.

Nothing at all. Common on half-tons without the tow package and on most SUVs. A hitch does not mean a brake controller. Plenty of trucks have a 7-pin socket wired for lights and power with the brake circuit never connected to anything.

The plug is a clue, not proof. A flat 4-pin has no brake circuit whatsoever. A 7-pin round has a brake pin, but somebody still had to wire it to an actual controller.

If you don't have one

You've got options, and none of them are cancelling.

Controllers aren't expensive. A basic proportional unit runs in the low hundreds installed, and less if you do it yourself — many trucks already have the factory connector sitting behind the dash waiting for one.

Or call me. I've helped renters sort this out before, including getting trailer brakes working on a truck that wasn't set up for them. I'd rather spend twenty minutes on that than watch you drive off with a trailer you can't stop properly, or cancel a job you were counting on.

What I'd ask is that you work it out before the morning you're leaving, not at five o'clock when you're already behind.

What Utah actually requires

This one gets misquoted constantly, so here's what the statute says rather than what gets repeated.

Utah Code 41-6a-1623 is written as a performance standard, not a weight cutoff. It requires that a vehicle, or a combination of vehicles, be able to stop within 40 feet from 20 miles per hour. There is no line in it saying trailers over some specific weight must have brakes.

You'll see a 3,000 pound figure quoted around the internet. That comes from federal standards that plainly apply to commercial vehicles. Whether they bind private towing in Utah is not something the sources I checked agree on, and I'm not going to pretend I've settled it.

The practical answer sidesteps the whole argument. My trailers have electric brakes. A controller is how you use them. Whatever the legal fine print says, a loaded trailer stops better with its brakes working, and that's the actual reason to care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which of your trailers need a brake controller?
Five of six. The 24ft enclosed, the 7x14 dump, the 8.5x22 car hauler, the 8.5x20 enclosed car hauler and the 8.5x22 equipment trailer all have electric brakes and require a working controller in your tow vehicle. The 7x14 utility trailer does not — it has a single 3,500 lb axle and a flat 4-pin plug.
How do I know if my truck already has a brake controller?
Look for a factory unit with a gain adjustment and a manual lever near the dash, common on 3/4-ton and 1-ton trucks. Otherwise look under the dash near your left knee for an aftermarket box with a dial. If you find neither, you probably don't have one.
Is a 7-pin plug the same as a brake controller?
No. The 7-pin round connector includes a brake circuit, but that circuit has to be wired to an actual controller to do anything. Plenty of trucks have a 7-pin socket wired only for lights and power.
What if I don't have a brake controller?
A basic proportional controller runs in the low hundreds installed, and many trucks already have the factory connector behind the dash. You can also call or text us at (385) 269-0712 — we've helped renters get trailer brakes working on trucks that weren't set up for them. Just sort it out before the morning you're leaving.
Does Utah law require trailer brakes?
Utah Code 41-6a-1623 is written as a stopping-performance standard rather than a weight threshold: a vehicle or combination must stop within 40 feet from 20 mph. It does not name a trailer weight at which brakes become mandatory. Regardless of the legal question, our trailers have electric brakes and a controller is how you use them.
Can I rent without a brake controller?
The 7x14 utility trailer, yes — it doesn't have electric brakes. For the other five, no, not safely. Call us before you book and we'll help you work out your options.
